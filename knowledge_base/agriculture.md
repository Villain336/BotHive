# Agriculture — Expert Knowledge Base

## Overview

Agriculture is the foundation of human civilization. It is the controlled cultivation of plants and animals for food, fiber, fuel, and other products. Modern agriculture feeds 8 billion people; its failure would collapse civilization within weeks. Understanding agriculture spans soil science, plant biology, water management, economics, policy, and technology.

---

## History of Agriculture

### The Neolithic Revolution (~10,000 BC)
- Agriculture independently developed in at least 12 regions worldwide
- **Fertile Crescent** (Middle East): wheat, barley, lentils, goats, sheep
- **China**: rice (Yangtze), millet (Yellow River)
- **Mesoamerica**: maize, squash, beans ("Three Sisters")
- **Sub-Saharan Africa**: sorghum, yams, pearl millet
- Shift from nomadic hunting/gathering to settled civilization
- Enabled population growth, specialization, cities, and complex societies

### Agricultural Revolutions
| Revolution | Era | Key Changes |
|-----------|-----|------------|
| Neolithic Revolution | ~10,000 BC | Domestication of plants and animals |
| Medieval Revolution | 900–1300 AD | Three-field rotation, heavy plow, horse collar |
| British Agricultural Revolution | 1700s | Enclosure, crop rotation (Norfolk 4-course), selective breeding |
| Green Revolution | 1950s–1970s | High-yield varieties, irrigation, chemical fertilizers/pesticides |
| Gene Revolution | 1990s–present | GMOs, precision breeding, CRISPR |

## Soil Science

### Soil Components
- **Minerals**: 45% — sand, silt, clay (texture determines water and nutrient holding)
- **Organic matter**: 5% — the critical, life-giving fraction
- **Water**: 25%
- **Air**: 25%

### Soil Texture Triangle
- **Sandy soil**: Drains fast; nutrient-poor; easy to work
- **Clay soil**: High water retention; nutrient-rich; compacts easily; poor drainage
- **Silt**: Between sand and clay; fertile; erosion-prone
- **Loam** (sand + silt + clay): Ideal for most crops; good drainage and water retention

### The Cation Exchange Capacity (CEC)
- Measure of soil's ability to hold positively charged nutrients (K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, H+)
- Higher CEC = more fertile potential
- Clay and organic matter have high CEC; sand has very low CEC

### Soil pH
- Scale 0–14; 7 = neutral
- Most crops: 6.0–7.0 (slightly acidic)
- Blueberries: 4.5–5.5 (very acidic)
- pH affects nutrient availability more than actual nutrient content
- **Lime** raises pH (acidic → neutral); **sulfur** lowers pH

### Soil Organic Matter (SOM)
- The foundation of soil health
- Built by: composting, cover crops, reduced tillage, manure, crop residue incorporation
- Benefits: water holding capacity, nutrient cycling, soil structure, microbial diversity
- Humus: the stable end product of decomposition; dark brown/black
- **1% increase in SOM** → can hold additional 20,000 gallons of water per acre

### The Soil Food Web
- Bacteria → Fungi → Protozoa → Nematodes → Microarthropods → Earthworms
- Mycorrhizal fungi: symbiotic relationship with 90% of plant species; extends root surface area 100–1,000x
- Earthworms: till, aerate, and enrich soil; Darwin spent 40 years studying them

## Plant Biology

### Plant Growth Requirements
- **Macronutrients (NPK)**:
  - **Nitrogen (N)**: Leaf growth; protein synthesis; chlorophyll; most limiting nutrient
  - **Phosphorus (P)**: Root development; flower/fruit; ATP (cellular energy)
  - **Potassium (K)**: Water regulation; disease resistance; fruit quality
- **Secondary macronutrients**: Calcium, Magnesium, Sulfur
- **Micronutrients**: Iron, Zinc, Manganese, Copper, Boron, Molybdenum (needed in tiny amounts)

### Photosynthesis
```
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
```
- **C3 plants**: Most crops (wheat, rice, soybeans); efficient in cool, moist conditions
- **C4 plants**: Corn, sorghum, sugarcane; more efficient in hot, sunny, dry conditions
- **CAM plants**: Cactus, succulents; open stomata at night to minimize water loss

### Plant Life Cycles
- **Annual**: Complete lifecycle in one growing season (corn, tomatoes, wheat)
- **Biennial**: Two years to complete (carrots, parsley)
- **Perennial**: Live for many years (fruit trees, asparagus, alfalfa)

### Seed Saving vs. Hybrid vs. GMO
- **Open-pollinated (OP)**: Breed true; seeds saved and replanted year after year
- **Heirloom**: Open-pollinated with history before 1951 (or 50+ years old)
- **Hybrid (F1)**: Cross of two stable varieties; high vigor but don't breed true from saved seed
- **GMO**: Genetically modified through transgenic insertion; patented; cannot be saved legally
- **CRISPR**: Gene-edited (deletions/modifications within genome); distinct from traditional GMO; regulation varies

## Farming Systems

### Conventional / Industrial Agriculture
- Mechanized; large scale; chemical inputs (synthetic fertilizers, herbicides, pesticides)
- High yield per labor unit; lower yield per acre than intensive alternatives
- Concerns: soil degradation, water pollution, monoculture vulnerability, carbon emissions

### Organic Agriculture
- No synthetic chemicals; biological pest management; crop rotation; composting
- USDA Organic certification: specific standards for inputs and practices
- Generally 20–25% lower yields than conventional for same crop
- Significantly higher price premium at retail (50–100%+)

### Regenerative Agriculture
- Beyond organic: actively rebuilds soil health and ecosystem function
- Principles: minimize tillage, maximize living roots, maximize diversity, integrate livestock, keep soil covered
- Practices: cover crops, no-till, silvopasture, rotational grazing, compost application
- Goal: sequester carbon, restore water cycle, rebuild soil organic matter

### Permaculture
- Permanent agriculture; design system modeled on natural ecosystems
- 12 principles including: observe and interact, use edges, catch and store energy, produce no waste
- Food forest: multi-layer system (canopy trees, understory, shrubs, herbs, ground covers, vines, roots)
- High diversity, low input once established

### Hydroponics / Aquaponics
- **Hydroponics**: Soil-less cultivation; roots in nutrient solution
- **Aeroponics**: Roots suspended in air; misted with nutrient solution (highest efficiency)
- **Aquaponics**: Fish produce waste → bacteria convert to plant nutrients → plants clean water for fish
- 10x more water-efficient than conventional; grows 10x faster in optimal conditions
- Challenges: energy cost, technical complexity, startup cost

### Vertical Farming
- Stacked growing layers in controlled environment
- LED lighting; climate control; hydroponic/aeroponic systems
- Location-independent; year-round production; zero pesticides needed
- Energy cost is primary economic challenge (~30–40% of operating cost)
- Profitable for: leafy greens, herbs, microgreens, strawberries

## Irrigation

### Water Use in Agriculture
- Agriculture accounts for ~70% of global freshwater withdrawals
- Irrigation is critical in arid and semi-arid regions

### Irrigation Methods

**Surface Irrigation (Flood/Furrow)**
- Oldest method; water flows over field by gravity
- Efficiency: 40–60% (much evaporates or runs off)

**Sprinkler Irrigation**
- Overhead spray; mimics rain
- Efficiency: 65–80%
- Risk: foliar disease from wet leaves

**Drip Irrigation (Microirrigation)**
- Water delivered directly to root zone through emitters
- Most efficient: 85–95% water use efficiency
- Enables fertigation (fertilizer applied through drip system)
- Higher upfront cost; superior long-term economics

### Water Harvesting
- **Swales**: Contour trenches that slow and sink water into the landscape
- **Ponds and reservoirs**: Store water for dry periods
- **Rainwater collection**: Cisterns from roof catchment
- **Keyline design**: System to maximize water infiltration across landscape

## Pest and Disease Management

### Integrated Pest Management (IPM)
1. **Prevention**: Crop rotation, resistant varieties, sanitation, timing
2. **Monitoring**: Scout fields; establish economic thresholds (spray only when pest exceeds threshold)
3. **Biological controls**: Beneficial insects (ladybugs eat aphids), parasitic wasps, nematodes, Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis) bacteria
4. **Chemical controls**: Last resort; select targeted, least toxic option

### Cover Crops
- Planted between cash crops; not harvested for sale
- Benefits: suppress weeds, fix nitrogen (legumes), build organic matter, prevent erosion, break pest cycles
- Common cover crops: clover (N fixation), rye (weed suppression), buckwheat (phosphorus mobilization), radish (deep tillage/"tillage radish")

### Companion Planting
- Three Sisters: corn (trellis) + beans (nitrogen fixation) + squash (ground cover/weed suppression)
- Marigolds: repel nematodes and some insects
- Basil + tomatoes: reputed companion benefit (some evidence for flavor improvement)

## Livestock Systems

### Rotational Grazing
- Animals moved frequently through paddocks; each paddock rests and recovers
- Prevents overgrazing; mimics natural predator-prey movement patterns
- Builds soil organic matter; sequesters carbon
- Mob grazing: extreme density/short duration version; highest intensity soil impact

### Animal Units and Carrying Capacity
- Stocking rate: Animal Units per acre; based on available forage
- 1 Animal Unit (AU) = 1,000 lb cow; adjust for other species
- Exceeding carrying capacity degrades land rapidly

### Pastured Poultry (Joel Salatin model)
- Chickens in mobile pens moved daily over pasture
- Eat insects and grass; supplement chicken feed; fertilize pasture
- High welfare; improved flavor; lower input cost when done at scale

## Agricultural Economics

### Farm Business Models
- **Commodity production**: Corn, soybeans, wheat — price-taker; compete on scale and efficiency
- **Specialty crops**: Organic vegetables, herbs, flowers, berries — higher margin; more labor
- **Direct marketing**: Farmers markets, CSA (Community Supported Agriculture), farm stands — highest margin; eliminates middleman
- **Agritourism**: U-pick, farm dinners, education — revenue without selling inputs

### Cost of Production
- Fixed costs: land (rent/mortgage), equipment, buildings
- Variable costs: seed, fertilizer, pesticides, fuel, labor
- Break-even analysis: must cover all costs; commodity prices make this difficult for small operations

### CSA (Community Supported Agriculture)
- Customers buy a "share" at season's start
- Farm receives upfront capital; customer receives weekly produce box
- Risk shifted to customer; farm has cash flow when it's needed most

## Food Security and Global Issues

- **Global food waste**: ~33% of all food produced globally is lost or wasted
- **Soil degradation**: ~33% of world's farmland is degraded; at current rates, topsoil could be exhausted in 60 years (FAO estimate)
- **Freshwater depletion**: Ogallala Aquifer (US Great Plains) — being depleted at 10–50x recharge rate
- **Climate change impacts**: Shifted growing zones; increased drought/flood frequency; CO2 enrichment (mixed effects)
- **Seed diversity loss**: 75% of crop genetic diversity lost since 1900 (FAO); Svalbard Global Seed Vault holds 1.3 million seed samples
